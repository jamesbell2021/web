04 — Grading

Why your grade isn't your grade yet

You'll be marked Pass, Merit or Distinction on each unit. The mark on your feedback sheet is the start of a process, not the end of one.

From your submission to your certificate

  1. Your tutor assesses it Written feedback within 15 working days, showing what worked and what to improve. This grade is provisional.
  2. {{iv|Internal Verification}} A second tutor reviews a sample of work from each unit to check the grading meets the standard. Grades can change here.
  3. The {{eqa|EQA}} visits Someone from Pearson — also called a Standards Verifier or Visiting Moderator — checks assessment decisions are fair and meet national standards. Usually March to June, in person or on Teams. They may run a learner focus group, so you might be asked your honest opinion of the course.
  4. The Assessment Board Reviews grades, extenuating circumstances, misconduct cases and progression. Three a year: Progress Board in January, Awarding Board in June, Supplementary Board in August.
  5. Ratified Only now is your grade final and your certificate issued.

What this means for you

Don't panic about a single mark, and don't bank on one either. If you think an assessment decision is wrong, raise it early with James rather than waiting for results day — there's an academic appeals process and you'll be told how to use it when you get your results.

Practice vs graded

Your assessment schedule shows both. Practice assignments exist so you can be bad at something safely and get told how to fix it. Graded assignments count. Know which is which before deciding how much effort to spend.

Reassessment

If you need to redo something you'll be told, and you'll submit over the summer. The August board confirms those results, usually in time for the {{ucas|UCAS}} deadline if you're applying to higher education.

09 — The brief

Build an arcade game

In your group of three, design and prototype an original arcade game with a retro style, then present it. You'll build a playable version using Gear Zero, and you'll keep a reflective log while you do it.

3

people

Threes where the numbers allow. Three is the size where everyone has a job and nobody hides. If the register doesn't divide neatly, one group of four is fine — that group splits the driver's job in two.

1

screen

Real arcade games fit on one screen with no scrolling story and no tutorial. That constraint is the point.

30

seconds

How long a stranger should need to understand your game. If it takes longer, it isn't an arcade game yet.

What "arcade" actually means

Arcade games were built to take a coin off you in a noisy room, from someone who had never seen the machine before. That produced a very specific set of design rules, and they're still some of the best design rules there are:

  • One {{mechanic|core mechanic}}. Jump. Shoot. Rotate. Catch. One verb, done extremely well.
  • Readable in a glance. You can tell what's dangerous, what's you, and what you want, without reading anything.
  • Instant start. No menus to fight through, no cutscene, no tutorial. You're playing within five seconds.
  • Difficulty that climbs. The first thirty seconds are easy on purpose. It gets harder until it kills you.
  • A score, and a reason to beat it. The loop is "again", and "again" has to feel like your fault, not the game's.
  • Short sessions. A run lasts a couple of minutes. Losing has to be quick enough that trying again is easier than walking away.

Retro style is a decision, not a filter

"Retro" isn't just pixel art because pixel art is easier. Pick a specific era and commit to it — the four-colour blockiness of early home computers, the black background and vector lines of an early cabinet, the chunky sprites and loud palettes of the mid-eighties, the pre-rendered look of the mid-nineties. Then be able to say why that era suits your game. That sentence is worth marks.

How to run it

  1. Pick your two slips From the nine on your table you've already chosen two that can live in the same game. Those are your seed. Write down why they work together.
  2. Write the one-sentence pitch Your whole game in a single sentence a stranger would understand. If you can't, the idea isn't clear enough yet. Rewrite it until you can.
  3. Describe it to {{gearzero|Gear Zero}} Type your pitch in plain words and let it build. You'll be playing something in minutes. It won't be right first time — that's the useful part.
  4. Iterate out loud Keep talking to it. Change one thing at a time so you can tell what each change did. Screenshot every version. Every prompt you write is a design decision and every rebuild is a result — that's your log entry writing itself.
  5. Decide what you're keeping At some point stop changing things and defend what you've got. Knowing when to stop is a skill nobody teaches and everybody needs.
  6. Present it Three minutes per group. Short enough that you have to know what matters about your own game.

The presentation

Cover these five things

  1. Your one-sentence pitch
  2. The era you chose and why it fits
  3. The core mechanic, demonstrated live
  4. One thing that changed between your first version and your last, and what made you change it
  5. What you'd do next if you had another week

Point four is the one that matters

Anyone can show a finished thing. Showing what you changed and why is the difference between describing and reflecting — and it's the same skill your log is marked on. Groups who skip it will be asked about it anyway.

Splitting the work

Three people, three jobs. Rotate them if you want, but agree who has which before you start — and put that in your log, because "we all just did a bit of everything" is where team projects go to die.

  • Driver — types the prompts, keeps the build moving, saves the versions.
  • Designer — holds the vision, decides what's in and what's out, defends the core mechanic when someone wants to bolt something on.
  • Recorder — screenshots each version, notes what changed and why, keeps the material the whole group needs for their logs.

Declare your AI use — this is a rehearsal for the real thing

You're using an AI tool to build this, openly and deliberately. That's fine. What matters is that you say so, and say what you did with it: what you asked for, what came back, what you changed, what you rejected. Every piece of assessed work you submit carries a declaration covering {{ai|AI use}}, and this task is a low-stakes run at getting that habit right. The College's AI guidance applies from today, not from your first graded assignment.

10 — Reflective log

What a reflective log actually is

You'll write one every day for two years. Most people write it badly for the first six months, then work out what it's for and wish they'd started properly. This page is an attempt to save you those six months.

It is not a diary

Nobody wants to know how you felt about Tuesday. "Today was good, we got a lot done" is worth exactly nothing.

It is not a to-do list

A list of what happened, in order, is a register. It records that you were present. It doesn't record that you were thinking.

It is a record of your decisions and what they cost you

Every day you make choices — which idea to chase, which tool to use, what to cut, who does what, when to stop. A reflective log captures those choices while you still remember why you made them, along with what happened as a result and what you'd do differently. That's it. That's the whole thing.

Why you're actually being asked to do it

  1. Because it's the evidence Your finished game shows what you made. It cannot show why you made it, what you rejected, or what you fixed. The log is the only place that exists. Without it, two of the five assessment criteria have nothing to mark.
  2. Because you will not remember In May, assembling a portfolio, you will not remember why you changed the enemy speed in October. Everyone believes they will. Nobody does.
  3. Because the grade descriptors are written about it The difference between a Pass and a Distinction is largely the difference between doing something and being able to explain the thinking behind it. That explanation lives in your log.
  4. Because it's a real industry habit Devlogs, {{postmortem|post-mortems}}, design documentation, commit messages, sprint retrospectives. Studios run on written reflection. You're not learning a college ritual, you're learning the job.

The rule that fixes most bad logs

Never write what you did without writing why

Every sentence about an action needs a sentence about the thinking. If you've written "we changed the background to black", you're not finished until you've written "because the sprites were getting lost against the blue and you couldn't tell what was dangerous".

Read your entry back. If you can add "so what?" after a sentence and there's no answer, that sentence isn't doing anything.

Three sentences, minimum, every time

1. What I did

Specific. Not "worked on the game" — which part, using what, for how long.

2. Why I did it that way

What were the options? Why this one? What did you rule out and on what grounds?

3. What happened, and what next

Did it work? How do you know? What's the next move because of it?

Write it in the last ten minutes of class

Not on Sunday night for the whole week. A log written from memory five days later is vague, because your memory is vague — and vague is exactly what loses marks. Ten minutes at the end of each session, while you can still remember what annoyed you, beats an hour at the weekend every single time.

You're reading this before you build, on purpose

Normally you'd learn this after the fact and spend the last ten minutes trying to remember what happened. Today you know first — so capture as you go. Keep a scrap of paper or an open document next to you and write one line every time you change something: what you changed, and why you changed it. Ten scribbled lines at 15:20 turn into a strong entry at 15:45. Nothing at 15:20 turns into "we made a game and it was good".

12 — Worked example

The same day, written three ways

One session on the arcade task: the group's first Gear Zero build came back wrong. Here's how three different students would write it up. Same day, same events, wildly different marks.

What changed between them

Not the amount of work. Not the quality of the game. All three students did exactly the same things that afternoon. The difference is entirely in whether they wrote down the thinking that was already in their head — and that thinking is free. You did it anyway. You just have to put it on the page.

You don't have to hit all five criteria every day

Some days are all technical work and nothing else. That's fine — write that day properly rather than inventing a communication paragraph. Across a fortnight, though, all five should appear. If one of them never does, that's a real gap in how you're working, not just a gap in your writing.
